A DATA 497 Data Narrative

Priced Out: When a Full-Time Wage No Longer Covers the Rent

Across the U.S., paychecks have grown — but not fast enough to keep up with the cost of a place to live. This is the story of the gap between what people earn and what it costs to keep a roof overhead.

About this project

The cost of living crisis is a result of wages stagnating over time. Across the United States, paychecks have grown, but not fast enough to keep up with the rising cost of everyday life. To support this narrative, we created three visualizations using data from various sources, all showing insight into the current economical situation.

Why this matters

The cost-of-living crisis is, at its core, a wage crisis. Prices have risen steadily for decades, but paychecks have not followed at the same pace, and for millions of Americans, that gap has become impossible to ignore. When inflation outpaces income growth year after year, people aren't just paying more for things; they're falling further behind. Nowhere is that clearer than in housing, where the gap between what a full-time worker earns and what it costs to rent a home has grown into one of the defining financial pressures of our time.

Visual 1 · Simon Mehari

Earning power has grown — but slowly

The unbroken green line tracks median income from its 1967 origin of $54,880 to an $83,730 peak in 2024. The steady, five-decade climb of the median American household income paints a picture of relentless financial progress, culminating in record high wages by 2024. Yet, raw income data tells only half the economic story since it serves as merely the baseline for a much more pressing question: how far does that income actually stretch in value?

The true measure of a household’s financial security is not only found in the sheer size of their gross income, but in its daily purchasing power. To understand the economic anxiety defining today’s cost of living crisis, yearly wage growth has to be tested against the realities of modern inflation for common goods and a rapidly escalating rent situation in nearly all states across the US. Ultimately, a record-high year of salary lessens in value if the economic price to sustain yourself climbs even faster.

Visual 2 · Manav Lakhani

Prices climbed faster than paychecks

Comparing average household income with the Consumer Price Index reveals the situation our economy is facing, and supports our hypothesis that stagnant wages are the real issue, not the increase of prices. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is a key indicator of inflation in our economy, measuring how much the average basket of everyday goods and services like groceries, rent, and gas, cost over time. Essentially, as the CPI increases, the cost of living increases as well.

When showing percentage increases since the year 2000, the gap between income and CPI is clear. While CPI has risen 82.2% since the baseline, wages have not been unable to keep up over the same period, ending at a 16.6% increase through 2024, showing how the gap between income and costs is bigger than ever before.

Visual 3 · Nikolai Decneut

How many hours must you work to afford rent where you live?

When local wages are plotted against housing costs in Washington and California, a clear gap emerges between standard pay rates with hours and actual rental prices. The housing wage needed to rent a basic home, while spending no more than 30% of income on rent, now significantly exceeds the minimum wage.

In Washington, affording a typical two-bedroom apartment requires an hourly wage of $34.47. At the state minimum wage, a person would need to work roughly 99 hours per week to meet this standard. In California, this requirement rises to nearly $50 an hour which is the equivalent of working nearly three full-time minimum wage jobs. Whether looking at urban centers or rural counties, the data shows a consistent trend that income necessary to afford standard housing has outpaced what many full-time workers earn. There is a clear disconnect between localized pay and local living expenses.

Conclusion

Our findings all point to the same conclusion: the affordability crisis facing Americans today is not simply a story of prices going up — it is a story of wages being unable or unwilling to follow. Taken together, the three visuals show a coherent story where nominal income growth, skyrocketing prices, and impossible to afford housing have made this one of the worst economies to live in. Rising costs are a normal part of any economy. What is not normal is when the paychecks meant to afford those costs fall further behind, making your money less valuable year after year. Until wages grow at a pace that keeps up with the true cost of living, millions of Americans will continue to work full time and still come up short.
